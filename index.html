Section 12.6

Why does an electron only get special orbits?

De Broglie's explanation of Bohr's second postulate of quantisation.

Bohr's model of the atom worked remarkably well. Yet its second postulate looked like a rule pulled from thin air. This chapter shows how matter waves turn that rule into a natural consequence.

The postulate

Bohr's second postulate

The angular momentum of the electron orbiting the nucleus is quantised. It can only take values that are whole-number multiples of h/2π.

Ln = nh2π,   n = 1, 2, 3, …

Why whole numbers only? Bohr did not say. The answer arrived about ten years later.

1913Bohr proposes his model with quantised angular momentum.
1923Louis de Broglie explains the quantisation using the wave nature of the electron.
1927C. J. Davisson and L. H. Germer verify the wave nature of electrons by experiment.

Chapter 2

Waves on a string: only some wavelengths survive

To understand the electron, first look at something you can pluck.

Pluck it

A vast number of wavelengths are excited on a string fixed at both ends.

Round trip

Each wave runs down the string, reflects, and comes back. That distance is 2L.

Only some stay

Waves with nodes at both ends form standing waves. The rest cancel themselves.

2L = Xλ   (X = 1, 2, 3, … for a standing wave)

In words: the round-trip distance must be one wavelength, two wavelengths, or any whole number of wavelengths. Any other wavelength meets itself out of step after reflection, and its amplitude quickly drops to zero.

Waves in the atom: a string with no ends

De Broglie pictured the orbiting electron as a wave travelling around the nucleus.

A circular orbit has no ends, but the idea is the same. After one trip around, the wave must meet itself exactly in step. The total distance travelled is the circumference, so for the electron in the nth orbit:

2πrn = nλ,   n = 1, 2, 3, … (12.12)

Fig. 12.8 in your textbook shows the case n = 4: four de Broglie wavelengths fit into the circumference, so 2πr4 = 4λ.

The big idea

Only resonant standing waves can persist. Orbits where the electron wave does not close on itself destroy themselves by interference. That is why the allowed orbits are quantised.

Chapter 4

Derivation: from waves to Bohr's postulate

Five short steps. Press the button to reveal each one.

What we just showed

The condition for a standing electron wave, 2πrn = nλ, together with λ = h/p, gives exactly the quantum condition Bohr proposed for angular momentum [Eq. 12.15]. This equation is the basis for the discrete orbits and energy levels of the hydrogen atom (Section 12.5).

So de Broglie's hypothesis explains Bohr's second postulate: the quantised orbits and energy states come from the wave nature of the electron, and only resonant standing waves persist.

Chapter 6

Where Bohr's model falls short

Bohr's picture treats the electron like a planet on a classical orbit. It predicts the gross features of hydrogenic atoms well, especially the frequencies of radiation they emit or absorb. It also has clear limits.

Hydrogenic atoms are atoms or ions with a single electron, such as H, He⁺ and Li²⁺.

Limitation 1

Only for hydrogenic atoms

Nucleus–electron attraction (included)Electron–electron repulsion (missing)

Limitation 2

Cannot explain line intensities

Bar heights are schematic. They show that some lines are strong and others weak.

Why limitation 1 happens

Bohr's formulation uses only the electric force between the positively charged nucleus and the electron. In an atom with more than one electron, each electron also feels the electric forces from all the other electrons. Attempts to extend the model along Bohr's lines to such atoms did not succeed, and it cannot even handle two-electron atoms like helium.

Why limitation 2 matters

Bohr's model gets the frequencies of the hydrogen lines right. In the emission spectrum, though, some visible lines are weak and others strong. Experiments show that some transitions are more favoured than others, and Bohr's model has no way to account for this.

The way forward

Bohr's model is an elegant picture, but it cannot be generalised to complex atoms. For those we need a new and radical theory, Quantum Mechanics, which gives a more complete picture of atomic structure.
